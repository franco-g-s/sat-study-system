# Sentence boundaries

*A closed rule set. There are exactly five legal ways to join two independent
clauses and the SAT does not invent a sixth — which is what makes this cheap:
finite, and once it is automatic it stays automatic.*

## The three-second cue

**The four choices differ only in punctuation.** A period against a semicolon
against a comma against a colon or a dash, sometimes with a conjunction attached.

That is the whole cue, and it means **stop reading for meaning.** Meaning is not
what is being tested here. Structure is.

## First, the only question that matters

**Is each side of the blank an independent clause?** Subject, *finite* verb,
could stand alone as a sentence.

*Having crossed the river at dawn* has no finite verb. It is a participial phrase, and
phrases never need a legal join.

<!-- PAGE -->

## Two independent clauses — five legal joins

| mark | name | when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **.** | full stop | always legal |
| **;** | semicolon | always legal — **identical in function to the full stop** |
| **:** | colon | only when the second clause explains or delivers on the first |
| **, +FANBOYS** | *, and · , but · , or · , nor · , for · , so · , yet* | the comma is **required** |
| **—** | em dash | behaves like the colon. Rarer, but legal |

### Illegal on sight

- **A bare comma.** Never joins two independent clauses. This is the comma splice
  and it is the most common wrong answer on the section.
- **Nothing at all** — a run-on.
- **A conjunction with no comma** — *…the lab closed and the staff went home…*
- **A comma plus a transition word, when it joins two independent clauses.**
  *However, therefore, moreover, consequently, nevertheless, thus, instead* are
  not conjunctions, so they cannot do a conjunction's job. `; however,` joins two
  clauses; `, however,` cannot.

<!-- PAGE -->

## But a comma before *however* is usually FINE

*The flat version of the rule on the previous page is wrong, and it is a common
point of confusion: "doesn't the rule say you can't have '..., however, ...'?"*

**It only bites when the transition word sits between two independent clauses.** When it is
a parenthetical inside ONE clause, paired commas are exactly right:

> The committee rejected the first proposal**, however,** judging that it
> underestimated the cost of the new bridge.

One independent clause — *The committee rejected the first proposal* — then a
participial phrase. *However* is a supplementary adverb, and supplementary elements take
**paired punctuation**. A semicolon would need a second independent clause after
it, and there is none.

### The test, and it takes three seconds

**Delete the transition word.** Are there still two independent clauses, one on
each side of the blank?

- **Yes** — it needs a real join, and a bare comma is a splice.
- **No** — the word is parenthetical, and **paired commas are correct**.

## The other three cases

### Dependent + independent

**A bare comma is correct here**, and a full stop would make a fragment. So more
punctuation is not safer — it is a different error.

A clause is dependent when it opens with a **subordinator** — *because, although,
while, when, if, since, after, before, unless, whereas* — or a **relative
pronoun** — *who, which, that*. Comma when the dependent clause comes first;
usually none when it comes second.

### A supplementary element

Paired punctuation, **of the same type**: two commas, two dashes, or two
parentheses. **Never one comma and one dash.** Check both ends, not the one at
the blank.

### No boundary at all

**A single comma never separates a subject from its verb**, however long the
subject is.

## The procedure

1. **Read to the full stop before choosing.** Always. **The sentence does not end
   at the blank.**
2. **Count the independent clauses across the whole sentence.**
3. Two of them → the blank must supply one of the five joins.
4. **A bare comma is never one. Eliminate it on sight.**
5. **If a choice would be right for a shorter version of the sentence, that is
   the trap working.** Check what comes after.

## The elimination almost nobody uses

**The semicolon and the full stop are functionally identical.** So if two choices
are a semicolon and a full stop doing the same job, **both are wrong** — the test
cannot have two correct answers. That kills half the options in three seconds
without parsing anything.

## Common trap — answering before the sentence ends

The most common way to miss these is to stop reading partway through the
sentence — at a phrase that sounds like an ending — and answer from there.

The grammar is not the problem. The right rule gets applied, correctly, **to a
sentence shorter than the real one**. Reading left to right and filling the blank
in as you go commits you early — fast, usually right, and precisely why it is
dangerous here.

**The checkable version, because it does not rely on noticing a feeling —**
**have I read to the period yet?**
